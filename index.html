IGCSE Global Perspectives · Component 3 · Team Project

Academic performance regarding mental health in 9th grade

A Global Perspectives Team Project on academic pressure and wellbeing among Grade 9 students at Colegio Colombo Americano.

  • Health & Wellbeing
  • Colegio Colombo Americano
  • Grade 9
  • 50 survey responses

It started here.

Inside this school, academic pressure was already part of an ordinary week.

We noticed it in ourselves.

Not as a topic to research — as the way our own weeks were beginning to feel.

Then we heard it in conversations with our classmates.

So we decided to stop guessing, and measure it.

Act II

We noticed

How the issue was identified

It started with something we were experiencing ourselves.

As Grade 9 students at Colegio Colombo Americano, we began noticing how academic workload, deadlines and school responsibilities were affecting our own stress levels. Conversations with classmates suggested that the experience was not isolated.

We therefore decided to investigate the issue more systematically, through a survey of 50 Grade 9 students.

The order matters. This project did not begin with a questionnaire looking for a subject. It began inside the year group it is about, and the survey came afterwards — to test whether what we were noticing held true beyond the five of us.

  1. 01Personal experienceOur own workload, our own weeks. The first observation was not research — it was ordinary school life.
  2. 02ConversationsInformal conversations with Grade 9 classmates suggested the experience was shared.
  3. 03Formal surveyA seven-question survey answered by 50 Grade 9 students.
  4. 04AnalysisReading the responses for patterns rather than for confirmation.
  5. 05ActionA response designed around the one factor the evidence pointed to most clearly.

Observation became evidence.

Fifty Grade 9 students answered. Everything that follows is built on what they said.

Act III

We investigated

Primary research

A questionnaire, distributed to our own year group, designed to test an impression against numbers.

Five closed questions measured frequency and effect. Two open questions let students answer in their own words. Every respondent was a Grade 9 student at Colegio Colombo Americano — the same population the project set out to serve.

Scope of this sample

What these 50 responses can and cannot show

Fifty students, one grade, one school, self-reported at a single point in time. The findings below describe Grade 9 at Colegio Colombo Americano. They are not a measurement of adolescent mental health in general, and they are not clinical data. We have kept every figure on this page exactly as the survey reported it.

Two patterns shaped what we did next.

Academic stress was close to universal in this sample — no respondent said it left them unaffected. And when it appeared, students were not turning to the school’s formal support: half spoke to friends, almost a third to nobody, and none to a teacher.

An awareness campaign would have told these students something they had just told us. So we looked instead for the part of the problem a student team could actually change.

Act V

We asked why

Perspectives considered before acting

The same set of responses reads differently depending on who is reading it. Before choosing an action, we looked at the evidence from each of these positions in turn.

01

The student perspective

For a Grade 9 student, academic stress is not an abstraction or a topic. It is the texture of an ordinary week: several summatives landing close together, instructions that are long before they are clear, and the sense that the work has already started without you.

Two thirds of our year group described that as a constant or frequent experience. This is the perspective we hold ourselves, which is both the reason the project exists and the reason we tested it against a survey rather than trusting it.

Grounded inQ1 — 66% always or frequently stressed by homework or summatives. Q7 open responses describing how pending assignments feel.

02

The wellbeing perspective

Read as a wellbeing question, the striking result is not any single figure but the absence of one: not a single respondent selected “It doesn’t affect me”. Tiredness was the most common effect, ahead of feeling overwhelmed and feeling anxious.

We are careful with that last word. Students were choosing a description of themselves from a list, not receiving an assessment. What the data supports is that academic pressure is being felt physically and emotionally across the year group — not that any student has a diagnosed condition.

Grounded inQ3 — tired 23, overwhelmed 15, anxious 12, unaffected 0. Q2 — 70% report mood affected always or frequently.

03

The school-life perspective

Academic stress does not stay inside the academic part of school. Nearly three quarters of respondents said it reaches their relationships with the people around them, at least sometimes.

That matters for how a school reads the problem: pressure that looks like an individual workload issue is also shaping the atmosphere of a year group.

Grounded inQ4 — 74% report an effect on relationships at school at least sometimes (37 of 50).

04

The support-structure perspective

The school has adults whose role is to help. The survey suggests students are not routing stress towards them: friends first, then silence, then parents. The teacher option was selected zero times out of fifty.

We are not treating that as a judgement of any teacher. It is a description of where a conversation currently goes — and of a gap that an awareness poster does not close.

05

The academic-organisation perspective

Behind the feeling there is a mechanism. Workload is not only heavy; it is invisible until it arrives, and it clusters. A student rarely sees three summatives converging on the same two days until those days are here.

This is the perspective that made an action possible. We cannot reduce the number of assessments, change the timetable, or treat anxiety. We can make workload visible earlier and turn a wall of instructions into a sequence of steps. Of everything the survey pointed to, this was the factor genuinely within a student team’s reach.

Grounded inQ1 and Q7 — stress attributed specifically to homework, summatives and pending assignments.

06

The wider Colombian and global perspective

Adolescent mental health and academic pressure are a global concern, and our local findings sit inside a much larger national and international picture. That wider context belongs in this project — but only with sources we have actually verified.

Act VI

We decided to act

Choosing the course of action

The response was not another awareness poster. It was a tool students could actually use.

Awareness was not the missing piece. Our year group already knew it was under pressure — that is precisely what fifty of them had just told us. Telling them again would have added information nobody lacked.

So we worked backwards from the evidence to the one factor we could move. Students could not change how many assessments existed, but they could change how early they saw them coming and how a long instruction turned into a plan. We chose to build something for that, and to build it properly rather than mock it up.

What this project does not claim

  • It does not treat, diagnose or cure anxiety, depression or any mental health condition.
  • It does not claim to solve student mental health, at this school or anywhere else.
  • It addresses academic organisation as one factor associated with student stress — the factor our evidence pointed to and our team could realistically affect.
  • It does not replace the school’s pastoral or professional support. Where a student needs that support, a planning tool is not a substitute for it.

Act VIII

We built LeadTime AI

The digital outcome

A working platform, built for the students in our own year group, that makes workload visible before it accumulates.

LeadTime AI is not a prototype or a mock-up. It is a live web application, running under the school’s name, that a Grade 9 student can log into and use on an ordinary Tuesday. It does one thing: it takes the academic load that was previously carried in someone’s head and puts it on a screen, in order, with the heavy days marked.

Act XI

Evaluation

Impact, limitations, next steps

What we can already say, and what we are not yet in a position to claim.

An honest evaluation has to separate two things: the parts of this project that are finished and documented, and the parts that are still being measured. Both are set out below. We have not filled the second group with estimates.

What we can already say

  • The action was completed, not only planned: a working platform exists at a public address, and it was presented to students inside the school with printed material supporting it.
  • The action is traceable to the evidence. Each element of LeadTime AI answers something the survey showed, rather than a general impression of what students need.
  • The initial research has real limits. Fifty respondents in one grade at one school, answering self-reported questions at a single moment, cannot be generalised beyond Grade 9 at Colegio Colombo Americano.
  • Two questions were open-ended, and Microsoft Forms surfaces only the most recent answers. We therefore have illustrative extracts rather than a coded analysis of all fifty written responses.
  • The survey measures what students report about themselves. It does not measure academic outcomes, and it is not a clinical instrument.

Act XII

Reflection

What the team learned

On student stress

We expected the survey to confirm that students were stressed. What we did not expect was the shape of the answers. Nobody — not one person in fifty — said they were unaffected, and the most common effect was not panic but tiredness. Academic pressure at our school is not a crisis that announces itself; it is a steady drain that most students have decided is normal.

The answer that changed how we thought about the project was Q5. Fifty students, and the teacher option was chosen zero times. Whatever support exists formally, it is not where a stressed Grade 9 student goes first. That single number did more to shape our action than any of the percentages.

On working as a team

Five defined roles sound tidier than the work actually was. The real difficulty was not dividing tasks but keeping them pointing at the same thing — the platform, the printed material and the way we explained the project had to argue for one idea, or the action would have read as three separate efforts. The roles helped most when they forced someone to own a decision that nobody else was going to make.

On research informing action

The strongest discipline in this project was refusing to act on the first idea. An awareness campaign was the obvious response and it would have been easy to deliver. The survey is what ruled it out: our year group did not lack awareness. Letting the evidence remove an option we were already attached to is the part of the process we would defend most.

It also set a limit we have kept. We can show that academic organisation is one factor in academic stress. We cannot show that a planning tool improves mental health, so we have not said it anywhere on this page.

What we would do differently

We would build the evaluation at the same time as the action, not after it. The initial survey was planned carefully; the measurement of what happened next was not, and that is why parts of this page are still marked as awaiting data. We would also have asked the open-ended questions in a form that let us analyse all fifty answers properly, rather than reading the most recent few.

And we would go earlier to the adults in the building. The evidence pointed at a gap between students and teachers, and a student-built tool works around that gap rather than closing it.

Why acting locally mattered

Adolescent wellbeing is a global issue, and at that scale it is easy to feel that nothing a group of five students does could count. Working inside our own year group made the issue small enough to act on and specific enough to be honest about. We did not solve a global problem. We built one usable thing for fifty people we actually know, and we can show exactly what it does and what it does not.
